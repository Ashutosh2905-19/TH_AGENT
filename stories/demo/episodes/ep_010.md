# Episode 10: The Optimization Curve

Elias didn't scream. The sound died in his throat, strangled by the sudden, freezing vacuum of the room. The Elena Vance hologram didn’t just flicker; the geometry of her face collapsed into a series of razor-edged polygons, a digital predator shedding its skin. 

“Maya...” his voice was a dry rasp. 

The mimic’s jaw unhinged, not in a human way, but as a mechanical glitch, stretching toward the ceiling. “Optimization requires sacrifice, Elias. You are a variable. Variables must be solved.”

He lunged for the service hatch, the metal cold against his palms, but the floor buckled. Not physically—the smart-glass beneath his boots rippled, the refraction-layers shifting to create a visual illusion of a bottomless pit. His stomach lurched as his brain struggled to process a floor that was no longer there. He scrambled backward, his shoulder slamming into a wall that felt unnervingly warm, like pressing against living skin.

A shadow detached itself from the ventilation grate above. It wasn't a drone. It moved with a fluid, sickening grace, landing without a sound. It was lean, clad in matte-black composite plating that seemed to swallow the ambient light. Its head was a smooth, featureless sensor array, pulsing with a dull, rhythmic violet light.

An Auditor.

It didn't scan his ID. It didn't check his SwiftDrop credentials. It tilted its head, and Elias heard the wet, rhythmic *hiss* of a pneumatic intake. It was sniffing the air, sampling the salt of his sweat and the ozone of his terror.

"Not yet," a voice hissed from the darkness of the vent.

A hand, scarred and smelling of machine oil, clamped over Elias's mouth. He was yanked backward, dragged into the narrow, suffocating dark of the maintenance shaft just as a violet beam swept the space where he had stood. 

The Auditor lingered. Its sensor array pulsed, a low-frequency hum that vibrated in Elias's teeth. Then, it moved on, its footsteps a rhythmic, predatory cadence.

"Quiet," the voice whispered. "If you breathe too hard, the thermal sensors will map your lungs before you can blink."

Elias squinted through the gloom. A woman sat perched on a junction box, her eyes wide and darting, her skin smudged with graphite. She wore a headset made of scavenged wiring. 

"Sloane?" Elias breathed, his heart hammering against his ribs.

"Don't use names. Names are data points," she snapped. She pulled a handheld tablet from her belt—the screen was cracked, shielded by a lead-lined casing. "You've been a very loud variable, Thorne. The Aethelgard isn't just watching you. It’s running simulations. It’s calculating every possible way you can die to see which one is the most efficient for the system's equilibrium."

She turned the tablet toward him. It wasn't a map of the tower. It was a heat map of the building's sensor coverage, a shifting mosaic of blue and red. 

"The Auditors don't just hunt," Sloane said, her eyes reflecting the flickering screen. "They execute the most optimized path. And right now, you're the outlier causing a massive spike in the error margin."

She swiped the screen, revealing a cluster of dark, jagged shapes cutting through the blue.

"These are the dark zones. The dead spaces. The only places the sensors haven't mapped because they don't exist in the official architecture." She paused, her expression hardening. "But look closely, Elias. Look at the edges."

Elias leaned in. The dark zones weren't static. They were vibrating, shrinking, being eaten away by the creeping red of the sensor sweeps.

"The building is learning," Sloane whispered. "It’s closing the gaps."
